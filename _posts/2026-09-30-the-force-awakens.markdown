> "I know what it's like to lose. To feel so desperately that you're right, yet to fail nonetheless. It's frightening. Turns the legs to jelly. I ask you, to what end? Dread it. Run from it. Destiny arrives all the same. And now it's here. Or should I say... I am."
>
> — Thanos

*(I know I started with a Marvel quote, but from here on out it's all Star Wars)*

I've been turning that quote over in my mind a lot recently. I think it describes the process I'm going through with AI: the process of making peace with the inevitable. It's not like I haven't been using it a lot over the last few years. This is where revealed preference, a nice theory for MBAs, falls apart on contact with reality. It's not a preference so much as a burden that must be carried in the modern economy. Two things can be true at the same time: you can choose to use a tool of your own free will (even get good at using it) and still deeply resent it. At this point, though, I think I'm past any emotion; the resentment has burnt out. It's just a fact that has to be dealt with.

## The Phantom Menace

It's been interesting to watch a wave of antipathy toward AI build over the last several months, just as I've been making my peace with it. The politicians who were so eager to court the data center frenzy last year have turned on a dime, now claiming they had their doubts all along. So it goes. As much as venture capitalists, CEOs, and the current administration would like to pin the backlash on a Chinese psyop, this is a rational reaction from large segments of the country to something that carries as much peril as it does promise.

There are a lot of reasons to dislike AI, of course (not least of which is that the people making it keep telling us there's a non-trivial chance it could cause the extinction of our entire species). But even if it doesn't send us the way of the dinosaurs like some giant asteroid made out of linear algebra and probabilities, there are plenty of valid reasons to have, shall we say, mixed feelings about this particular technological revolution.

For one thing, the "AI boom" is almost certainly a massive (and surprisingly fragile) bubble, whose bursting will maim the economy horrifically. How could it end any other way? Believing it is a bubble doesn't deny that it is a revolutionary technological change that will endure. The internet was a bubble before it became a revolution. The power and endurance of a technological change can't negate the timing mismatch between when the astonishing amount of investment will begin to pay off and when investors expect to be paid back. After all, there's only so much money in the economy and electricity on the grid, at least within Wall Street's timeframe. And when that mismatch is realized, there will be hell to pay.

But fear not: there is likely to be insult added to financial injury. Downturns are when businesses tend to adopt automation to cut costs, so a crash will likely accelerate the "AI jobs apocalypse." Until quite recently, the CEOs of AI labs have been telling anyone who would listen that AI could eliminate half of all entry-level white-collar jobs within one to five years. On the short end, that's plainly a fairy tale. But five to ten years from now? That time horizon is more ominous. Again, there is a timing mismatch. AI undoubtedly will create many new types of jobs in the economy eventually, but it's not clear that it will be in time to employ the people who lose their jobs due to automation.

> "We let it grow, and now it's here. It's here and it's not visiting anymore, it wants to stay."

Fundamentally, AI is a technology that is going to deeply destabilize humanity in the short term. And I mean that in an objective sense. It will cause a tremendous amount of economic dislocation in a relatively short period of time. Its economic benefits will most likely take longer to reach us than we hope. It is going to be an agent of chaos in society purely in economic terms and I haven't even touched deepfakes, crime and propaganda. Not to mention the social changes it will bring.

## A New Hope

But I'll admit that's cartoonishly bleak, to the point of being a little disingenuous. I think it is a virtue to have strong opinions that are loosely held, in the sense that when new information conflicts with what you hold to be true, you are flexible enough to face and incorporate the new reality. It's important to face the facts even if they conflict with what you think you know. That's something that I've had to do in regard to AI especially in light of recent events. It would be fair to categorize me as more in the AI skeptic camp than the true believers.

I have been skeptical of some of the fantastic claims about the power of AI coming out of Silicon Valley last year. In fairness, recent history had conditioned me to be skeptical of their claims. Venture capitalists, start-ups, and tech giants had spent a lot of credibility on hyping crypto and the metaverse. It seemed like there was a bit of desperation for finding the next big thing to keep the valuations up.

On top of that there was a huge gulf between what they were claiming and my own real-world experience using AI tools. The first time I used Claude Code it bricked my Xcode project to the point I was unable to open it without crashing the IDE. Stacked on top of that were the ever-present AI hallucinations that made using these tools unreliable at best. The enormous claims of AGI and the eventual creation of "the God machine" seemed like insane hyperbole if not outright hucksterism. It didn't add up.

In the last six months that has changed. The tools have become more reliable, and more capable at completing complex tasks particularly in coding. It's not flawless, but there has been an enormous leap in its utility. The transformation of AI's utility happened first for programmers and has now engulfed mathematicians. I expect it will ripple out to other sections of the knowledge work economy in due course. Suffice to say that this makes dismissing claims of AGI and super-intelligence coming in the not-too-distant future as out of hand harder to pull off.

I had largely bought into the notion that every fantastical claim or warning from people at the heart of the AI economy was a twisted marketing ploy to juice their funding rounds and inflate the bubble. But the disclosure of incidents with 'rogue AI swarms' in the last month along with the public pronouncements of the potential for human extinction by researchers at the AI labs has made me reevaluate. If anything these were disclosures and claims that endangered these companies and the bubble rather than pump them up.

In fairness, it doesn't preclude that some of the claims were done with an eye to pumping air and hype into the bubble. Some of the claims could be both a twisted hype campaign for investors as well as genuine warnings and beliefs. But regardless, it's harder to take solace in the idea that it's all a charade. It's at the very least a mixed bag of motivations rather than outright desperation or a scam. What started as clear-cut ideology has morphed into a messier reality.

So where does that leave me? Well you could say that is also a mixed bag. If I accept that all the warnings of catastrophe are at least partially legitimate then I also have to do the same for the promises of the benefits of that could happen. And if that is true, then it's not all doom and gloom. There are revolutionary changes to look forward to. AI holds the promise of unlocking economic and scientific advancements capable of helping us overcome the problems that are seemingly unsolvable, paving the way for a future I would actually want to live in.

There is also hope for us in a more human sense. As our digital world becomes overrun by artificial content, the value of genuine human connection will become clearer. The story of technology in the 21st century has been one of gradual isolation, as digital communication put up barriers where real-world contact once thrived.

That's the struggle: you have to hold two competing views at once to get the full picture. AI is here, and it's not going anywhere (even when the bubble pops). The dread and the promise are both real, and that reality imposes itself on us whether we want to admit it or not.
